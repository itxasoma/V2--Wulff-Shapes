\\begin{abstract}
This report uses the spherical cluster approximation (SCA) to
study how the size and shape of a nanopore affect the fraction of
atoms lining its interior surface and the density of the host
material. Two pore geometries are considered: spherical and cubic.
In both cases an analytical expression for $\\Fpore = \\Nporesurf/\\Nremain$
is derived and compared with the fraction $\\FS$ of surface atoms in the
corresponding free nanoparticle. The key result is that $\\FS$ and $\\Fpore$
show opposite trends with pore size: $\\FS$ decreases as $N$ grows
(approaching the bulk limit), whereas $\\Fpore$ increases and diverges
as the pore fills the unit cell. The cubic geometry gives surface fractions
$\\approx 24$\\% larger than the spherical one, a direct consequence of the
greater surface area of a cube relative to a sphere of equal volume. The
code used for the analysis is available in a public GitHub repository
(see code section).
\\end{abstract}

%====================================================================
\\section{Introduction}
\\label{sec:intro}
%====================================================================

Nanoporous materials such as zeolites and metal-organic frameworks
(MOFs) are characterised by a network of pores at the nanoscale
that gives them extraordinarily high surface areas per unit
volume.~\\cite{Yaghi2003,Kitagawa2004} The large fraction of atoms
that are exposed at pore surfaces is precisely what makes them
useful for gas storage, catalysis, and molecular
separation.~\\cite{Eddaoudi2002}
A central question is therefore how this surface fraction depends
on the size and shape of the pores, and what happens to the
density of the host material as the pore volume grows.

In this report we address both questions using the same simple
geometric framework introduced in Class~2 for free nanoclusters:
the spherical cluster approximation (SCA).~\\cite{Bromley2025}
The idea is to form a nanopore by removing a cluster of $N$ atoms
from the centre of a cubic unit cell containing $\\Nbox$ atoms, and
to compute the fraction $\\Fpore = \\Nporesurf/\\Nremain$ of pore
surface atoms with respect to the atoms that remain in the cell.
We compare this quantity with the surface fraction $\\FS$
of a free cluster of the same size, and we repeat the analysis for
both spherical and cubic pore shapes. The limit $R = L/2$, at which
adjacent pores in the periodic cell just touch, is studied as a
special case.

%====================================================================
\\section{Employed Models \\& Methodology}
\\label{sec:methods}
%====================================================================

\\subsection{Spherical cluster approximation}
\\label{sec:sca}

The SCA models an $N$-atom cluster as a sphere.~\\cite{Bromley2025}
By equating the volume of the sphere to $N$ times the volume of
one atom (radius $r_a$) one obtains
\\begin{equation}
    R = r_a\\,N^{1/3},
    \\label{eq:radius}
\\end{equation}
where $R$ is the cluster radius.
The number of surface atoms is then estimated as the surface
area of the cluster divided by the cross-sectional area of one atom,
\\begin{equation}
    \\Nsurf = 4\\,N^{2/3},
    \\label{eq:nsurf}
\\end{equation}
so that the fraction of surface atoms in a free spherical cluster is
\\begin{equation}
    \\FS = \\frac{\\Nsurf}{N} = 4\\,N^{-1/3}.
    \\label{eq:FS}
\\end{equation}
For a cubic cluster of the same volume the surface area is 24\\%
larger than for a sphere, which gives a prefactor of
$1.24\\times 4 = 4.96$:
\\begin{equation}
    \\FS^{\\text{cube}} = 4.96\\,N^{-1/3}.
    \\label{eq:FScube}
\\end{equation}

\\subsection{Spherical nanopore}
\\label{sec:sph_pore}

A spherical nanopore of $N$ atoms is formed by removing a
spherical cluster from the centre of a cubic box.
The interior surface of the pore is identified with the surface
of the removed cluster, so
\\begin{equation}
    \\Nporesurf = 4\\,N^{2/3}.
    \\label{eq:nporesurf_sph}
\\end{equation}
The number of atoms remaining in the box after the pore is created
is simply
\\begin{equation}
    \\Nremain = \\Nbox - N,
    \\label{eq:nremain}
\\end{equation}
and the pore surface fraction is therefore
\\begin{equation}
    \\Fpore^{\\text{sph}} = \\frac{\\Nporesurf}{\\Nremain}
    = \\frac{4\\,N^{2/3}}{\\Nbox - N}.
    \\label{eq:Fpore_sph}
\\end{equation}
This expression is valid for $0 < N < \\Nbox$. At the limit
$R = L/2$, i.e.\\ $N \\to \\Nbox$, the pores from neighbouring unit
cells just touch. At this point $\\Nremain \\to 0$, meaning the host
material has been entirely removed and the density of the porous
material, $\\rho_{\\text{porous}} = \\Nremain/L^3$, goes to zero.

\\subsection{Cubic nanopore}
\\label{sec:cub_pore}

For a cubic pore the same construction applies, but the prefactor
of $\\Nporesurf$ becomes 4.96:
\\begin{equation}
    \\Fpore^{\\text{cube}} = \\frac{4.96\\,N^{2/3}}{\\Nbox - N}.
    \\label{eq:Fpore_cube}
\\end{equation}
At $R = L/2$ the density goes to zero by the same argument as in
the spherical case: removing a cube with side $L$ from a box of
side $L$ leaves nothing behind.

\\subsection{Computational details}
\\label{sec:comp}

All curves were computed with $\\Nbox = 100$ and
$N \\in [1, \\Nbox - 0.5]$ sampled at 2000 points.
The diverging tails of $\\Fpore$ near $N \\to \\Nbox$ were clipped
at $F = 10$ for readability in the plots.
The code is written in Python~3 using NumPy and Matplotlib and is
available in the GitHub repository linked at the end of this report.

\\subsection{Exercise 1 -- spherical pore and nanoparticle}
\\label{sec:res_sph}

Figure~\\ref{fig:plot1c} shows $\\Fpore^{\\text{sph}}$ and $\\FS$ as a
function of $N$ for $\\Nbox = 100$. The two curves cross early and
then diverge in opposite directions, which is the most important
qualitative result of this exercise.

\\begin{figure}[h!]
    \\centering
    \\includegraphics[width=\\linewidth]{figures/plot_1c.pdf}
    \\caption{$\\Fpore$ and $\\FS$ as a function of $N$ for the
    spherical geometry ($\\Nbox = 100$). The pore fraction diverges
    as $N \\to \\Nbox$, while $\\FS$ decreases monotonically toward
    the bulk limit. The curve of $\\Fpore$ is clipped at $F = 10$.}
    \\label{fig:plot1c}
\\end{figure}

$\\FS = 4\\,N^{-1/3}$ decreases as $N$ grows because a larger
cluster has a smaller fraction of its atoms at the surface; in the
bulk limit $N \\to \\infty$ it goes to zero.
By contrast, $\\Fpore$ increases with $N$: a larger pore has more
surface atoms ($\\propto N^{2/3}$) but leaves fewer atoms in the
box ($\\Nremain = \\Nbox - N$), so the ratio grows without bound
as $N \\to \\Nbox$.

Figure~\\ref{fig:plot1d} shows the same quantities plotted against
$N^{-1/3}$.

\\begin{figure}[h!]
    \\centering
    \\includegraphics[width=\\linewidth]{figures/plot_1d.pdf}
    \\caption{$\\Fpore$ and $\\FS$ as a function of $N^{-1/3}$ for the
    spherical geometry ($\\Nbox = 100$). $\\FS$ is exactly linear
    [Eq.~(\\ref{eq:FS})], while $\\Fpore$ curves upward due to the
    $({\\Nbox - N})$ denominator.}
    \\label{fig:plot1d}
\\end{figure}

$\\FS$ is a perfect straight line through the origin in this
representation, as expected from Eq.~(\\ref{eq:FS}).
$\\Fpore$ is not: the denominator $(\\Nbox - N)$ introduces a
non-linearity that makes the curve bend upward as
$N^{-1/3} \\to 1$ (i.e.\\ $N \\to 1$ would give a small pore, but
$N \\to \\Nbox$, i.e.\\ $N^{-1/3} \\to 100^{-1/3} \\approx 0.215$,
is where the divergence happens). This is the key difference
between a free nanoparticle and a nanopore in a periodic host:
their surface fractions share the same numerator but diverge in
behaviour because of the finite size of the box.

\\subsection{Exercise 2 -- cubic pore and nanoparticle}
\\label{sec:res_cub}

Figures~\\ref{fig:plot2N} and \\ref{fig:plot2Ninv} show the
analogous results for the cubic geometry, with the spherical
curves overlaid as a reference. The shape of the curves is
identical to the spherical case, but all values are shifted upward
by a factor $4.96/4.0 = 1.24$. This is consistent with the known
result from Class~2 that a cube has 24\\% more surface area than a
sphere of equal volume.~\\cite{Bromley2025}

\\begin{figure}[h!]
    \\centering
    \\includegraphics[width=\\linewidth]{figures/plot_2N.pdf}
    \\caption{$\\Fpore$ and $\\FS$ as a function of $N$ for the cubic
    geometry ($\\Nbox = 100$), with the spherical results shown as
    thinner lines for comparison. The cubic curves lie uniformly
    above the spherical ones by a factor of 1.24.}
    \\label{fig:plot2N}
\\end{figure}

\\begin{figure}[h!]
    \\centering
    \\includegraphics[width=\\linewidth]{figures/plot_2Ninv.pdf}
    \\caption{$\\Fpore$ and $\\FS$ as a function of $N^{-1/3}$ for the
    cubic geometry ($\\Nbox = 100$). As in Fig.~\\ref{fig:plot1d},
    $\\FS^{\\text{cube}}$ is linear while $\\Fpore^{\\text{cube}}$
    curves upward near large $N$.}
    \\label{fig:plot2Ninv}
\\end{figure}

At the limit $R = L/2$ the density of the porous material goes to
zero for both geometries, since $\\Nremain \\to 0$. In practice this
limit is never reached in real nanoporous materials: the host
framework must retain enough atoms to remain mechanically stable,
so there is always a minimum wall thickness that prevents the
pores from touching.

\\subsection{Comparison and physical implications}
\\label{sec:comparison}

The central comparison of this exercise is between $\\FS$ and
$\\Fpore$. In a free nanoparticle, the surface fraction decreases
with size, which is why nanoparticles converge to bulk behaviour
as $N$ grows. In a nanoporous material the opposite is true: a
larger pore exposes more internal surface while consuming
progressively more of the host material, so $\\Fpore$ grows with
$N$.

This has a direct practical implication. In applications where
the surface reactivity of the pore walls matters (catalysis,
adsorption, drug delivery), the optimal pore size is not the
largest possible one, because a very large pore would also
compromise the mechanical integrity of the framework by removing
too much material.~\\cite{Yaghi2003,Eddaoudi2002} The model
presented here captures this trade-off in a transparent way,
even if it is only an approximation.

Regarding limitations, the SCA model treats atoms as hard
spheres and ignores atomic ordering and packing fractions
entirely. In a real zeolite or MOF, the pore surface is not
smooth: it is made of specific atomic sites (bridging oxygens,
metal nodes) that interact with guest molecules in a
chemistry-dependent way. The SCA estimates are therefore useful
as order-of-magnitude bounds, but not as quantitative
predictions of real pore surface fractions.~\\cite{Kitagawa2004}

%====================================================================
\\section{Conclusions}
\\label{sec:conclusions}
%====================================================================

In this report the SCA was extended to the case of a spherical and
a cubic nanopore carved out of a periodic cubic box.
The pore surface fraction $\\Fpore = \\Nporesurf/\\Nremain$ and the
free-particle surface fraction $\\FS$ share the same numerator but
show opposite dependences on pore size: $\\FS$ decreases as
$N^{-1/3}$ toward zero, while $\\Fpore$ increases without bound as
$N \\to \\Nbox$.

In the $N^{-1/3}$ representation, $\\FS$ is exactly linear
[Eq.~(\\ref{eq:FS})], while $\\Fpore$ curves upward because of the
finite-box denominator $(\\Nbox - N)$.
Both effects are amplified by a factor of 1.24 when the
geometry is changed from spherical to cubic, reflecting the
larger surface area of a cube relative to a sphere of equal
volume.

The limit $R = L/2$, where adjacent pores just touch in the
periodic lattice, corresponds to a density of zero in both
geometries: all atoms have been removed from the box.
This is the maximum possible porosity in this simple model,
and it represents a useful upper bound for the void fraction
of a real nanoporous material.
